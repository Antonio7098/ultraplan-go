# Dimension 01.11: Terminal Outcome Arbitration

## Purpose

Study how a runtime selects one authoritative terminal outcome when success, failure, cancellation, timeout, panic, and internal termination signals compete.

## Steps

1. Identify every path that can propose or commit a terminal outcome.
2. Distinguish commands, observations, causes, candidate outcomes, and committed outcomes.
3. Determine whether arbitration uses first-writer wins, explicit precedence, fact resolution, event order, or another rule.
4. Locate the serialization point for competing terminal signals.
5. Inspect how cancellation-related errors are distinguished from unrelated failures.
6. Check whether success can be committed after cancellation is requested, accepted, or observed.
7. Determine whether a committed terminal outcome is immutable.
8. Check whether losing terminal candidates and their causes remain observable.
9. Test whether arbitration meaning is deterministic under different scheduler interleavings.

## Evidence to Capture

- Terminal state and outcome types
- Completion, failure, cancellation, timeout, and panic handlers
- Compare-and-swap, lock, channel, or single-writer arbitration points
- Outcome precedence tables or transition guards
- Cancellation request, acknowledgement, and observation metadata
- Error and cause matching logic
- Race, stress, and terminal-transition tests
- Logs or events for losing terminal candidates

## Questions

1. Which facts can compete to terminate a run?
2. Who is authorized to commit the terminal outcome?
3. Is the winner selected by timing, precedence, or resolved execution facts?
4. Is cancellation a command, fact, cause, outcome, or several distinct concepts?
5. Can success follow requested, accepted, or observed cancellation?
6. How are cancellation-related failures separated from unrelated failures?
7. Can a committed terminal outcome be amended?
8. Are losing candidates retained for diagnosis?
9. Can scheduler order change the semantic result?

## Rating

| Score | Meaning |

## Executive Summary

Five agent harnesses answer "when does the system ask a human?" with five different scopes of ambition. **langgraph** ships pure mechanics: a checkpoint-backed interrupt/resume primitive plus declarative node gates, deliberately delegating every *semantic* trigger condition (risk, uncertainty, budget) to application code (`libs/langgraph/langgraph/types.py:851-974`). **openai-agents-sdk** goes deep on one semantic — tool risk — with a uniform, fail-closed, identity-scoped approval engine evaluated centrally during tool planning (`src/agents/run_internal/tool_planning.py:759-840`). **agent-framework** builds the broadest taxonomy: eight trigger classes (per-tool flags, curated provider defaults, MCP allowlists, plan-review uncertainty, security-policy violations, server-initiated sampling, declaration-only tools, budget fallback) funneled into two transport primitives (`python/packages/core/agent_framework/_tools.py:1763`; `_workflows/_functional.py:196-253`). **crewai** has no automatic triggers at all; every review point is statically declared by a developer via task flags, training mode, a flow decorator, or hook-context requests (`lib/crewai/src/crewai/task.py:233-236`; `lib/crewai/src/crewai/hooks/tool_hooks.py:86-128`). **openhands**, as a product frontend, compiles two user settings into an explicit wire-level confirmation policy (`NeverConfirm` / `ConfirmRisky(HIGH, confirm_unknown)` / `AlwaysConfirm`) and delegates enforcement to its server (`src/api/agent-server-adapter.ts:593-605`).

Three findings cut across all sources. First, everyone converged independently on **interrupt-and-resume over blocking prompts**: no source blocks a thread awaiting a human; all pause durably and resume from serialized state. Second, **the human's decision is systematically under-audited** — four of five sources explicitly flag the absence of a decision-level audit record (who approved what, when, why), making this the dimension's most consistent gap. Third, **budget/limit exhaustion almost never routes to review**: it produces errors (langgraph `GraphRecursionError`, openai-agents-sdk `MaxTurnsExceeded`), pauses (openhands), or at best a fallback-to-human inside auto-approval loops (agent-framework .NET).

Ratings cluster tightly (7–8): the mechanics of pausing are mature everywhere they are attempted; what separates scores is trigger breadth, evaluation safety, and audit depth.

## Core Thesis

HITL triggering in these harnesses has split into two independently maturing layers: **transport** (how a pause travels — durable pending state, strict request/response binding, resumable serialization) and **policy** (what causes a pause — risk, uncertainty, violation, budget). The transport layer is converging on a shared best-practice design visible in every high-scoring source: persist the open request, bind responses to recorded request identities, reject forged/stale answers, resume deterministically. The policy layer has *not* converged: it ranges from "none" (langgraph, crewai) through single-dimension tool risk (openai-agents-sdk) to multi-class layered triggers (agent-framework) to user-facing compiled policies (openhands). The practical lesson: transport patterns are safely copyable today; policy design remains a per-product decision driven by trust model and user shape — and the missing piece nobody ships is a unified, append-only decision ledger that would make either layer operationally trustworthy.

## Approach Models

Four distinct approach models emerge across the five sources:

1. **Generic interrupt primitives (mechanics-first)** — langgraph. The framework exposes exactly two trigger families: compile-time node lists (`interrupt_before`/`interrupt_after`, `libs/langgraph/langgraph/graph/state.py:1183-1184`) and an imperative in-node `interrupt(value)` callable usable under any developer-authored condition (`types.py:851-974`). Everything risk-shaped lives in application code; the framework guarantees only that pauses are durable, addressable, and replayable.

2. **Centralized tool-approval engines (single-semantics-first)** — openai-agents-sdk, and the core loop of agent-framework. Every planned tool call funnels through one evaluation point (`tool_planning.py:798-838` in openai-agents-sdk; `_tools.py:1763-1790` in agent-framework) that consults a per-tool rule (`needs_approval` bool/callable; `approval_mode` literal), stops the run before side effects, and surfaces structured approval items. agent-framework extends this vertically with middleware (standing rules, auto-approval callbacks, queuing — `_harness/_tool_approval.py:343-379`) while openai-agents-sdk extends it horizontally across seven tool surfaces (function, shell, apply_patch, custom, agent-as-tool, local MCP, hosted MCP — `src/agents/tool.py:486-1499`).

3. **Layered heterogeneous trigger taxonomy (platform-first)** — agent-framework's full picture. Beyond tool approval, triggers fire on provider-curated defaults (skills tools registered `always_require`, `_skills.py:1864-1878`), MCP hosted-tool allowlists (`_mcp.py:1704-1718`), orchestration stall/uncertainty (Magentic plan review after replans, `_magentic.py:1185-1186`), security policy violations configured as approve-on-violation (`security.py:2077-2083`), deny-by-default server-initiated sampling (`_mcp.py:1434-1462`), non-executable declaration-only tools (`_tools.py:1836-1843`), and auto-approval budget exhaustion falling back to a human (.NET `ToolApprovalAgentOptions.cs:50-68`). All converge on two transports: `function_approval_request` content and workflow `ctx.request_info()` suspension.

4. **Declared review points & compiled policies (product-first)** — crewai and openhands. crewai's triggers exist only where developers place them: a post-answer task flag (`task.py:233-236`), force-enabled training mode (`crew.py:927-932`), a metadata-stamping `@human_feedback` decorator executed by the flow engine (`flow/dsl/_human_feedback.py:23-57`), and hook-context `request_human_input()` calls for arbitrary gates. openhands translates two settings (`confirmation_mode`, `security_analyzer`) at conversation start into one of three named policies sent on the wire (`agent-server-adapter.ts:593-605`); enforcement belongs entirely to the agent-server, and the frontend renders the resulting wait state.

These models are not quality tiers — they are responses to product shape. A low-level graph runtime cannot predefine risk semantics without guessing wrong; a frontend repo should not re-implement enforcement; a platform spanning workflows, orchestrations, and security middleware needs more trigger classes than a single-agent SDK.

## Pattern Catalog

### P1. Interrupt-and-resume over blocking prompts
- **Problem**: humans respond in seconds or days; blocking execution threads wedges servers.
- **Sources**: all five. agent-framework suspends workflows via `ctx.request_info()` and resumes via `run(responses={...})` (`_functional.py:196-253`; `_workflow.py:1011-1043`); langgraph raises `GraphInterrupt` persisted as checkpointer writes (`pregel/_runner.py:585-591`); openai-agents-sdk returns control with `RunResult.interruptions` and resumes from serialized `RunState` (`result.py:541-563`); crewai returns a `HumanFeedbackPending` value with persisted context (`flow/async_feedback/types.py:148-218`); openhands streams `WAITING_FOR_CONFIRMATION` over WebSocket (`use-agent-state.ts:24-25`).
- **Why it works**: decouples human latency from process lifetime; enables cross-process, cross-device, cross-queue resumes.
- **Copy when**: any HITL surface exists. **Overkill when**: purely interactive CLI tools where blocking `input()` is honest about its semantics (crewai's default providers do exactly this and pay for it in headless deployments).
- **Evidence**: citations above; crewai's cross-process resume test (`test_async_human_feedback.py:936-1036`) proves durability.

### P2. Strict response↔request binding with forgery rejection
- **Problem**: approvals answered out-of-band, replayed, or fabricated must not execute tools.
- **Sources**: agent-framework requires boolean-exact grants and drops unbound responses (`_tools.py:1975-1977`, `:2182-2213`); DevUI rejects client approvals for unknown server-issued ids (`devui/_executor.py:742-802`). openai-agents-sdk detects reused call ids for different invocations with `ModelBehaviorError` (`run_context.py:344-352`). langgraph rejects ambiguous multi-interrupt resumes without an ID map (`pregel/_loop.py:916-919`). crewai raises on unknown pending-feedback ids (`runtime/__init__.py:1248-1249`).
- **Why it works**: makes the set of acceptable answers closed and session-recorded, so stale/forged input fails loudly instead of executing.
- **Copy when**: any approval can arrive from a network, queue, or serialized store. **Risky when skipped**: any host that lets clients submit approval payloads directly.

### P3. Fail-closed trigger evaluation at trust boundaries
- **Problem**: a broken or absent evaluator must not silently approve.
- **Sources**: openai-agents-sdk treats checker exceptions as approval-required (`tool_planning.py:806-812`) and forces `True` for callable MCP policy lacking agent context (`mcp/server.py:815-846`). agent-framework denies sampling requests when the callback raises (`_mcp.py:1451-1458`). openhands sets `confirm_unknown: true` so unclassifiable actions still prompt (`agent-server-adapter.ts:601`).
- **Why it works**: converts evaluator bugs into availability cost rather than safety incidents.
- **Copy when**: evaluating third-party tools, LLM-derived risk, or anything adversarial. **Cost**: a buggy predicate turns every call into a pause (explicitly noted as an openai-agents-sdk tradeoff).

### P4. Scoped standing approvals ("don't ask again")
- **Problem**: repeated identical prompts train users to click through.
- **Sources**: agent-framework persists standing rules scoped by tool or tool+exact arguments and by hosted `server_label`, so approving one MCP server never approves a same-named tool on another (`_harness/_tool_approval.py:109-111`, test at `test_harness_tool_approval.py:1224`). openai-agents-sdk stores sticky decisions scoped by approval scope, with exact-call decisions overriding stickies and namespaced tools never falling back to bare-name decisions (`run_context.py:644-661`; tests `test_run_context_approvals.py:30,531`).
- **Why it works**: convenience without cross-boundary privilege bleed.
- **Copy when**: approval frequency is high and tools are stable. **Avoid when**: tool identity is mutable (name collisions documented as a hazard in both sources — `_tool_approval.py:365-376`).

### P5. Batch-pause semantics
- **Problem**: partially-approved batches create ambiguous side-effect states.
- **Sources**: agent-framework classifies whole model batches first; if any call needs approval nothing executes and safe siblings hide in session state until resume (`_tools.py:1775-1832`). openai-agents-sdk stops the run before any side effect when any call is pending (`turn_resolution.py:885-911`).
- **Why it works**: preserves "no partially-authorized execution" as an invariant. **Cost**: latency and serialization of multi-tool turns (agent-framework relaxes this after .NET auto-approval caps, `ToolApprovalAgentOptions.cs:32-34`).
- **Copy when**: tools in one batch interact or share resources.

### P6. Programmatic auto-decision escape hatches
- **Problem**: some approvals should be decided by code, not people, without losing record-keeping.
- **Sources**: agent-framework's `auto_approval_rules` callbacks evaluated before prompting (standing rules → heuristics → prompt, `_tool_approval.py:351-379`); openai-agents-sdk's `on_approval` callbacks applied through the same `approve_tool`/`reject_tool` path as humans (`run_internal/tool_execution.py:1191-1213`).
- **Why it works**: separates "who decides" from "how it is recorded"; keeps one ledger path.
- **Caution**: name-based matching carries collision blast radius — both frameworks document rather than prevent it.

### P7. Uncertainty and stall escalation (rare)
- **Problem**: agents stuck in loops or facing ambiguity need human arbitration, not more retries.
- **Sources**: agent-framework alone implements this among automatic triggers: Magentic plan sign-off on enablement and again with `is_stalled=True` after stall-reset (`_magentic.py:956-958`, `:1185-1186`). openhands handles epistemic uncertainty coarsely via the analyzer's UNKNOWN bucket counting as risky. Contrast: langgraph's recursion limit raises an error instead (`errors.py:67-83`); crewai's guardrail failures exhaust retries then raise (`task.py:1376-1384`).
- **Copy when**: orchestration-level progress signals exist to key off. **Absent evidence elsewhere**: no other source found confidence- or failure-count-driven review triggers.

### P8. Compile configuration into explicit named policies
- **Problem**: ad-hoc booleans scattered across layers drift from intent.
- **Sources**: openhands reduces the entire trigger space to three named kinds on the wire contract, asserted verbatim against upstream semantics by tests (`__tests__/api/agent-server-adapter.test.ts:349-376`); agent-framework uses small literal vocabularies (`ApprovalMode`, `MCPSpecificApproval`) similarly.
- **Why it works**: small, greppable vocabulary survives protocol evolution and cross-team negotiation.
- **Cost**: expressiveness ceiling — openhands' HIGH threshold is hardcoded precisely because the taxonomy is fixed (`agent-server-adapter.ts:601`).

## Key Differences

**Trigger breadth is the sharpest divergence.** agent-framework fires review from eight condition classes including uncertainty and policy violations; openai-agents-sdk restricts itself to tool risk ("Not implemented as triggers: model uncertainty, token/budget exhaustion, guardrail violations" — its report, citing `strict_schema.py:126` and inline guardrail rejection); langgraph and crewai ship zero built-in semantic conditions; openhands covers risk severity plus two non-confirmation interventions (budget pause, user pause). This tracks product scope: platforms accumulate trigger classes because they span more surfaces where different risks arise; focused SDKs stay narrow because generality would push policy invention onto users anyway.

**Where policy lives differs structurally.** Library sources (langgraph, openai-agents-sdk, agent-framework) evaluate triggers inside the execution loop, so hosts inherit behavior but configure it per tool/node. openhands inverts this: the client *compiles* configuration into a declarative contract and the server owns enforcement (`agent-server-adapter.ts:1120-1121`) — clean separation, but the frontend can only display truth it receives, leaving a stale-socket trust gap it acknowledges. crewai splits even further internally: its Crew/Task stack and Flow stack are parallel HITL implementations sharing almost no code or audit surface.

**Default posture is inconsistent and consequential.** agent-framework defaults bare local tools to `never_require` (`_tools.py:408`) while curating conservative defaults only inside trusted providers and network boundaries — a deliberate our-code/their-server asymmetry. openai-agents-sdk defaults `needs_approval=False` everywhere, keeping org-wide policy an application concern. openhands defaults confirmation *off* but the analyzer *on*. langgraph and crewai make no default at all because they make no automatic promise. None of the three configurable-posture sources chooses deny-by-default for local tools; all reserve conservatism for boundaries they don't control.

**Budget/limit handling reveals philosophy.** The same event (limits reached) becomes a human fallback in agent-framework .NET (`ToolApprovalAgentOptions.cs:63-65`: final invocation without auto-approve so remaining requests reach a person), an intervention-pause in openhands (`use-handle-ws-events.ts:55-61`), and a hard error in langgraph (`GraphRecursionError`) and openai-agents-sdk (`MaxTurnsExceeded`). Only the first two treat limits as "time to involve a human"; the error-raising sources treat them as bugs in configuration.

**Audit depth diverges most where mechanisms multiply.** crewai's Flow path has excellent typed events, telemetry spans, and checkpointable event types (`events/types/flow_events.py:244-287`) while its Task path records only a telemetry boolean — the same repo contains both best and near-worst audit practice. agent-framework audits well within each mechanism but admits "no single cross-cutting HITL decision ledger." langgraph persists everything mechanically yet captures no decision metadata (responder identity, rationale). openhands' decision POSTs to a runtime endpoint with no corresponding event type in the stream.

## Tradeoffs

- **Generality vs guidance** (langgraph vs openai-agents-sdk). langgraph gives bulletproof mechanics and zero policy — teams invent their own risk taxonomies and nothing stops an ungated dangerous-tool path. openai-agents-sdk gives one strong semantic with uniform evaluation but misses spend, failure-count, and confidence moments its own report lists as natural review points. Comparative verdict: mechanics-first scales to unforeseen needs; semantics-first gets safe defaults faster.
- **Expressiveness vs auditability** (agent-framework vs openai-agents-sdk). agent-framework's many trigger classes each carry their own audit story (session snapshots, checkpoints, security audit log, telemetry) — rich but fragmented. openai-agents-sdk's single engine keeps records uniformly but only in mutable run context with no independent log. Neither achieves unified decision audit; they fail differently.
- **Static declaration vs runtime sensitivity** (crewai vs everyone). Declared-everywhere guarantees review happens where written and is introspectable (`skill.py:427` hides HITL fields for visualization) but is blind: a destructive tool runs ungated unless a developer wrote the hook, and guardrail exhaustion crashes without escalation (`task.py:1376-1384`). Every dynamic-evaluation source pays more evaluation complexity to close exactly this hole.
- **Blocking simplicity vs deployment fit** (crewai defaults vs its own Flow path). Default `input()` providers wedge headless/server deployments; crewai's own provider abstraction solves this for Flows (remote providers, timeouts, async persistence) but the Crew/Task path lacks the equivalent — the same project demonstrates both the right pattern and the cost of not applying it universally.
- **LLM-judged routing vs deterministic outcomes** (crewai flow collapse). Free-form feedback collapsed to routing outcomes by structured outputs is ergonomic, but misreads silently reroute and the ultimate fallback is `outcomes[0]` — list order becomes policy (`runtime/__init__.py:3636-3639`). No other source delegates trigger *interpretation* to an LLM; others keep humans deciding discrete questions.
- **Client-configured vs enforced posture** (openhands). Compiling settings server-side yields a tiny wire vocabulary and schema-driven UI with dependency ordering (`depends_on`), but the threshold is unconfigurable from the client and the AlwaysConfirm fallback may be noisy enough that users disable confirmation entirely — the safest default becoming the least used.

## Decision Guide

Choose an approach model by answering three questions:

1. **Do you need arbitrary mid-execution suspension, or just tool gating?**
   - Arbitrary suspension → langgraph-style dual-track interrupts or agent-framework's `request_info` primitive. Both require checkpoint/state persistence; langgraph additionally demands idempotent node bodies up to the interrupt point (replay-on-resume semantics, `types.py:864-868`).
   - Tool gating only → centralized evaluation engine (openai-agents-sdk / agent-framework core loop) gives one semantic across all tool surfaces with less code than per-node hand-rolling.

2. **Who defines risk — you or your users?**
   - You do (library/platform vendor) → build a small named-policy vocabulary like openhands' three kinds, with fail-closed unknown handling; resist parameter sprawl until users demand thresholds.
   - Your users do → expose per-tool predicates (bool-or-callable as in openai-agents-sdk) plus scoping rules for standing decisions; consider a registry mapping annotations to automatic gates (both openai-agents-sdk and langgraph reports propose this as future work).

3. **What happens when limits hit?**
   - If your products have attendable humans → route to review (agent-framework .NET fallback, openhands pause-with-resume).
   - If unattended → fail loudly with typed errors (langgraph/openai-agents-sdk) rather than silently continuing; never adopt crewai's current guardrail behavior (exhaust retries → raise, no escalation option) without adding an escalate path.

Composition rule observed in practice: layer policy atop primitives rather than choosing one. agent-framework is the existence proof — its eight trigger classes all ride two transports — though its fragmented audit shows the integration cost.

## Practical Tips

- **Persist the open request before surfacing it**, keyed by a stable identity. Copy agent-framework's immutable pending-request snapshots with duplicate-id rejection (`_tools.py:2141-2157`) or langgraph's INTERRUPT writes (`_runner.py:585-591`). This single choice enables forgery rejection, crash recovery, and audit reconstruction later.
- **Make grant acceptance strict.** Require exact boolean `True` (agent-framework `_tools.py:1975-1977`) and bind responses to recorded request ids; accept nothing positional when more than one interruption is outstanding (langgraph `_loop.py:916-919`).
- **Scope every standing/"always" decision** by trust boundary (server label, namespace, approval scope), not tool name alone — both implementing sources found cross-scope bleed worth dedicated tests (`test_harness_tool_approval.py:1224`; `test_run_context_approvals.py:531`).
- **Fail closed on evaluator exceptions**, and say so in docs: openai-agents-sdk's exception→approval-required rule is the cheapest safeguard in the study (`tool_planning.py:806-812`).
- **Give rejected calls model-visible feedback** with customizable text (openai-agents-sdk `resolve_approval_rejection_message`, `tool_execution.py:1230-1244`) — rejection then steers the agent instead of dead-ending it.
- **Bundle complete disclosure into one approval request**: agent-framework waves all detected violations together so approving one cannot silently skip another (`security.py:1889-1918`); openhands shows the risk banner before the click (`conversation-confirmation-buttons.tsx:107-118`).
- **Emit typed lifecycle events around every prompt**, as crewai's Flow path does (`HumanFeedbackRequestedEvent`/`ReceivedEvent` with correlating request_id) — retrofitting this onto a mechanism that lacks it (as crewai's Task path must) is far harder than emitting from day one.
- **Add timeouts to interactive review loops early**: only crewai's flow `ask()` supports them (`runtime/__init__.py:3453-3470`); the task loop waits forever on `input()`. Long-running hosts will need expiry semantics that none of the five currently provides.

## Anti-Patterns / Caution Signs

- **Two parallel HITL stacks in one product** (crewai Task vs Flow): duplicated concepts with subtly different behavior (iteration rounds only on one; outcome routing only on the other) and wildly different audit depth. Caution sign: reviewers asking "which review UX is this?".
- **String-matching error copy to detect limit events** (openhands matches the prefix "Agent reached maximum", `use-handle-ws-events.ts:57`): fragile coupling between server prose and client policy; demand typed error codes.
- **Fallback-by-list-order**: any design where an ambiguous or failed interpretation lands on `options[0]` (crewai collapse fallback) turns ordering accidents into routing decisions.
- **Documented-but-unprevented collision hazards**: both auto-approval systems warn that name-based matching can over-match (`_tool_approval.py:365-376`) — if your safety property depends on a docstring, it isn't a property yet.
- **Volatile client-side idempotency**: openhands' double-submit dedup lives in a non-persisted store (`event-message-store.ts:15`), so correctness silently shifts to unverifiable server behavior after reload.
- **Stale-docstring drift on HITL APIs**: agent-framework's Magentic docs reference type names that don't exist (`_magentic.py:1527` vs actual `:806`) — in the highest-stakes API surface, documentation rot is a leading indicator that trigger contracts lack tests.
- **Silent remapping of unknown states**: openhands maps `STUCK` → `ERROR` (`use-agent-state.ts:30-31`), hiding exactly the state a human should see.
- **Opt-in safety on local code**: when risky-by-default is the developer's burden (bare `@tool`, `needs_approval=False`), the caution sign is any onboarding path that creates tools without mentioning approval flags.

## Notable Absences

Consistent across multiple sources:

- **Unified decision ledgers.** Nobody ships an append-only record of who approved/rejected what, when, and why spanning all trigger types. agent-framework: "no single cross-cutting HITL decision ledger." openai-agents-sdk: no approval spans exist in tracing at all (`grep approval src/agents/tracing/` returns nothing). langgraph: no responder identity in checkpoint metadata (`base/__init__.py:38-86`). openhands: the decision endpoint has no corresponding event type. crewai: hook prompts leave no trace.
- **Timeout/expiry on pending requests.** agent-framework notes pending approvals remain resumable indefinitely; crewai's primary loop has no timeout; openai-agents-sdk re-prompts indefinitely if applications resubmit unresolved items.
- **Budget-as-confirmation triggers.** Budgets exist as display (openhands `max_budget_per_task` is render-only), schema validation (openai-agents-sdk `strict_schema.py:126`), or errors — nowhere as a review trigger except agent-framework .NET's auto-approval iteration cap.
- **Composable trigger predicates.** openai-agents-sdk accepts exactly one bool/callable per tool (`util/_approvals.py:32-51`); crewai's flag is a bare boolean; langgraph leaves composition to node code. Only agent-framework's middleware layers standing rules + heuristics + prompts, and even there argument-aware *trigger* predicates ("require approval when path starts with /etc") are absent — proposed as future work.
- **Agent-initiated help-seeking.** crewAI explicitly forbids it (`AskQuestionTool` targets coworker agents only, `ask_question_tool.py:14-28`); no other source exposes a first-class "ask the human" verb available to the model itself — all triggers are developer/host-configured.

## Per-Source Notes

- **agent-framework (8)** — Most complete trigger taxonomy and the only source with uncertainty/stall escalation and policy-violation-as-approval. Formal spec governs the approval resume contract with scenario-to-test mapping (`docs/specs/004-python-function-calling-loop.md:17-18,119-122`); 23 dedicated test cases cover forged approvals, mixed batches, streaming. Weaknesses: opt-in approval for plain tools, fragmented audit, stale docstrings, no Go implementation.
- **crewai (7)** — Cleanest declaration model and the study's best async pause/resume ergonomics (auto-persist, cross-process resume, trace continuity via `execution_uuid`). Its Flow-path event/telemetry/checkpoint integration is a model worth copying; its Task path shows what omitting those costs. No automatic triggers whatsoever.
- **langgraph (8)** — The mechanics reference implementation: deterministic content-addressed interrupt IDs, version-vector anti-re-trigger gating, ambiguity-rejecting resumes, proven interruption without state updates. Choose it as the substrate pattern; bring your own policy.
- **openai-agents-sdk (8)** — The deepest single-vertical: uniform evaluation across seven tool surfaces, identity-scoped sticky decisions with mismatch detection, nested-run propagation, realtime parity, custom rejection feedback to the model. Its fail-closed rules are the study's most consistently applied safety discipline; its audit absence is the study's most instructive gap.
- **openhands (7)** — Best evidence for the product/frontend slice: compiled named policies, unknown-risk conservatism, multi-sensory attention routing, input locking, schema-driven settings. Enforcement lies outside the repo boundary, so server-side claims (idempotency, decision persistence) are explicitly unverifiable from this source.

## Open Questions

- Does agent-framework's hosted Foundry/CopilotStudio surface enforce independent approval triggers? Not verifiable from source (`docs/specs/004-python-function-calling-loop.md:62` asserts ownership only).
- Do openhands' servers treat repeated `respond_to_confirmation` calls idempotently, and do they persist decisions as events? Outside the analyzed repo boundary.
- Would langgraph's proposed policy registry (annotations → automatic `interrupt_before` insertion) and openai-agents-sdk's proposed global trigger policy converge on the same design if implemented? Both reports propose nearly complementary features; neither has evidence.
- Is crewai's enterprise/platform layer richer than the OSS trigger surface? The feedback-config `metadata` field hints at platform integration but holds only developer data in this source (`flow/human_feedback.py:170`).
- Should budget exhaustion route to humans or error? The sources disagree by philosophy; no operational evidence in any report indicates which produces better outcomes at scale.

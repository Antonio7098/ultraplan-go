## Executive Summary

Six sources split cleanly into two families that answer the dimension's central question differently. For the four agent-facing sources (agent-framework, langfuse, openai-agents-sdk, openhands), "approval" means **pausing execution until a human decides**, and the engineering effort goes into making that pause durable, forge-proof, and narrowly scoped. For the two infrastructure sources (opa, temporal), there is no human in the loop at all — temporal's tree contains *zero* occurrences of "approval" (`grep` over the entire source, per its report) — and the effort goes into evaluating a policy against every request before any handler runs (`v1/server/authorizer/authorizer.go:107-165` for opa; `common/authorization/interceptor.go:129-185` for temporal). Langfuse bridges both families: static RBAC gates availability exactly like temporal's role matrix, then an approval lifecycle suspends the agent mid-run (`packages/shared/src/in-app-agent/server/runLifecycle.ts:222-397`).

Scores span 6–9 (mean ≈7.8), and the spread tracks one variable more than any other: **how tightly an approval decision is bound to the exact operation it authorizes, and what happens when that binding fails**. The two 9s (agent-framework, openai-agents-sdk) rebuild every incoming response against an immutable recorded call before honoring it — agent-framework re-binds responses to stored request snapshots so call id/name/arguments cannot be substituted (`python/packages/core/agent_framework/_tools.py:2182-2213`), and openai-agents-sdk rejects decisions lacking a bindable invocation-ledger entry (`src/agents/run_context.py:938-941`). Openhands scores lowest not because its pipeline is untyped — it is traceable end to end (`src/api/agent-server-adapter.ts:593-618`) — but because its approval wire contract is a bare `accept: boolean` on "the last agent event" with memory-only duplicate protection (`src/api/event-service/event-service.types.ts:1-4`; `src/stores/event-message-store.ts:6-20`), and actual enforcement lives outside the studied repo.

Three convergent findings hold across all six: decisions fail closed where parsing is strict (non-boolean approvals deserialize as rejection in agent-framework, `python/packages/core/agent_framework/_types.py:1459-1461`; undefined Rego decisions produce HTTP 500 in opa, `v1/server/authorizer/authorizer.go:134-138`); grants are narrow by default with broader stickiness as an explicit opt-in; and **no source fully solves expiry or revocation of standing grants** — every report lists some variant of "no TTL" as a residual risk.

## Core Thesis

**A permission gate's strength is determined at the moment a decision arrives, not when the policy is authored.** The highest-rated sources treat every incoming approval response as hostile input that must be re-derived from trusted state: the decision carries only identifiers plus a boolean, the tool identity and arguments are replayed from persisted records, and any mismatch fails loudly rather than executing. The lower-rated paths accept the decision closer to face value.

Four observations support this:

- **Binding quality predicted ratings better than policy expressiveness.** opa has the most expressive policy language in the study (arbitrary Rego over the full request document, `v1/server/authorizer/authorizer.go:169-227`) yet rates 8, held back partly because its own authorization decisions are never recorded and its input contract was historically corrupted by typos until schema type-checking caught them (`v1/runtime/runtime.go:573-577`, motivated by `internal/compiler/utils_test.go:85-96`). Meanwhile openai-agents-sdk's policy vocabulary is just bool-or-callable per tool (`src/agents/tool.py:486-493`) yet rates 9 because decisions are anchored to a SHA-256 invocation fingerprint (`src/agents/_tool_invocation.py:236-289`) with adversarial regression tests for reuse and drift (`tests/test_tool_approval_call_id_reuse.py:270-298`).
- **The same failure class appears wherever binding is loose.** agent-framework documents that its auto-approval rules match tools *by name*, so a caller-configured colliding tool name would be silently auto-approved (`python/packages/core/agent_framework/_harness/_file_access.py:1375-1382`); openhands' pending-action heuristic attaches confirm/reject to "the last agent-sourced event" regardless of which event paused (`src/components/shared/buttons/conversation-confirmation-buttons.tsx:30-36`). Both are instances of trusting a label where an identity is needed.
- **Defaults mattered as much as mechanisms.** agent-framework flips its own dangerous surfaces to `always_require` (`python/packages/core/agent_framework/_harness/_file_access.py:1444-1474`) while temporal ships a noop claim mapper that grants `RoleAdmin` to everyone when unconfigured (`common/authorization/claim_mapper.go:52-54`). Identical interceptor architecture; opposite risk posture.
- **Durability forced the hardest engineering.** Every source supporting long-running work had to serialize pending authority — transcript contents (agent-framework), versioned RunState snapshots (openai-agents-sdk, four of seventeen schema bumps are approval-specific, `src/agents/run_state.py:186-217`), or Postgres rows with exactly-once CAS (langfuse, `runLifecycle.ts:235-355`) — and each paid for it in replay/forgery defenses that the stateless infrastructure sources simply never need.

## Approach Models

- **agent-framework — approvals as first-class transcript content behind a layered control plane.** Two content types (`function_approval_request`/`function_approval_response`, `python/packages/core/agent_framework/_types.py:373-374`) flow through the function-calling loop alongside ordinary tool calls. A minimal per-tool `ApprovalMode` literal lives in the loop itself (`python/packages/core/agent_framework/_tools.py:106`); richer behavior — standing rules with argument/server scoping, queued multi-request handling — is an opt-in `ToolApprovalMiddleware` requiring session state (`python/packages/core/agent_framework/_harness/_tool_approval.py:343,381-385`). Independent further layers add information-flow policy enforcement (FIDES-style taint tracking, `security.py:1643-1704`), a deny-by-default MCP sampling gate with rate limit (`_mcp.py:1434-1548`), and a transport-edge AG-UI lifecycle where the *server* owns approval authority inside a capacity-capped state machine (`python/packages/ag-ui/agent_framework_ag_ui/_approval_lifecycle.py:93-119,236-265`). A maintained spec declares this area extra-validation territory and maps ~30 scenarios to named tests (`docs/specs/004-python-function-calling-loop.md:31-35,445-474`).

- **langfuse — RBAC decides what exists; approval decides what runs.** A static role→scope matrix (~50 project scopes, `packages/shared/src/features/rbac/projectAccessRights.ts:5-91`) filters tool availability before the model sees anything; a compile-time-exhaustive registry then classifies every MCP tool `"auto"` or `"approval"` (`packages/shared/src/in-app-agent/server/mcpPolicy.ts:27-360`). Approved-pending runs park as durable interrupts; decisions commit through a compare-and-swap that also pushes any standing grant into the same transaction (`runLifecycle.ts:235-355`). The stated principle holds in code: approval never widens what the user could already do manually (`web/src/features/in-app-agent/README.md:231`), enforced by rebuilding the policy from current role every run (`worker/src/features/in-app-agent/executeInAppAgentRun.ts:335-344`).

- **opa — the engine polices itself.** When enabled, every request is answered by evaluating a user-authored Rego policy at `/system/authz/allow` before any handler (`v1/server/server.go:793-814`), with identity injected by pluggable Bearer/TLS middleware (`v1/server/identifier/token.go:22-33`). The distinctive move is meta-level hardening: the shape of the security decision's input document is frozen in a JSON schema enforced by Rego type-checking at startup *and* on every bundle activation (`v1/runtime/runtime.go:573-577`; `v1/bundle/store.go:997-1001`), and even the config file is validated by an embedded Rego policy (`v1/config/validate.rego:18,29-31`). Policy supply is gated cryptographically via signed bundles with mandatory key-scope match (`v1/bundle/verify.go:70-116,199-208`).

- **openai-agents-sdk — interrupt-and-resume with a canonical invocation ledger.** Any of five tool kinds opts in via `needs_approval` (static bool or per-call callable, `src/agents/tool.py:486-493`); gated calls surface as `ToolApprovalItem`s in `RunResult.interruptions` (`src/agents/items.py:556-623`). Decisions live in one centralized ledger on the run context (`src/agents/run_context.py:89`) as dual-mode records — sticky booleans bound to a fingerprinted authorization scope, or exact call-ID lists (`run_context.py:56-68`) — with deterministic conflict rules (approve wins ties, exact beats sticky, `run_context.py:649-658`). Everything serializes inside the versioned RunState format so paused runs survive restarts (`src/agents/run_state.py:1300-1324`).

- **openhands — client-side policy compilation, thin approval UX.** Flat user settings (`confirmation_mode`, `security_analyzer`) are compiled at conversation start into SDK discriminator objects — `NeverConfirm`, `AlwaysConfirm`, or `ConfirmRisky{threshold:"HIGH",confirm_unknown:true}` (`src/api/agent-server-adapter.ts:593-605`) — and stamped into the launch payload (`agent-server-adapter.ts:1120-1121`). Which actions pause is decided server-side using a per-action LLM-assessed `security_risk` enum (`src/types/agent-server/core/events/action-event.ts:58-61`); the frontend reacts to `WAITING_FOR_CONFIRMATION` status with Confirm/Reject buttons authenticated by session key (`src/api/event-service/event-service.api.ts:40-69`). Enforcement lives in a separate repo, explicitly flagged by the report.

- **temporal — authorization as a stateless plugin seam.** An `Authorizer` interface receives claims + a `CallTarget{API, namespace, request}` and returns Allow/Deny per request (`common/authorization/authorizer.go:24-56`); a hand-maintained metadata map tags every API with required scope and access level (`common/api/metadata.go:70-208`); gRPC interceptors enforce before handlers (`common/authorization/interceptor.go:129-185`). Nothing about a decision persists: revocation equals credential expiry (`claims.Valid()` at `default_jwt_claim_mapper.go:194`), JWKS key rotation (`default_token_key_provider.go:113-136`), or pushing new policy data.

## Pattern Catalog

### P1. Interrupt-and-resume with serializable approval state

- **Problem:** human approval latency is unbounded; the run must survive process restarts, deploys, and streaming/non-streaming duality without losing or duplicating authority.
- **Demonstrated by:** agent-framework (approvals persist as transcript contents resumable across processes, `python/packages/core/agent_framework/_types.py:1273-1361`); openai-agents-sdk (versioned RunState snapshots, compatibility corpus test guarding released contracts, `tests/test_run_state_compatibility_corpus.py`); langfuse (parked `AWAITING_APPROVAL` row + durable Mastra interrupt parsed by a shared parser across browser/web/worker, `packages/shared/src/in-app-agent/interrupts.ts:7-37`).
- **Why it works:** the pending question becomes ordinary durable state, so crash recovery is the same mechanism as normal resume; all three pair it with binding defenses (P2) because persistence creates replay opportunities.
- **Copy when:** agents execute side-effecting tools and humans respond asynchronously. This is effectively mandatory for production HITL.
- **Overkill/risky when:** single-process interactive CLIs; the cost is real — agent-framework's invocation file alone is 3,694 lines and its spec warns edits can "replay stale approval authority" (`docs/specs/004-python-function-calling-loop.md:31-33`).

### P2. Zero-trust decision payloads bound to canonical invocation identity

- **Problem:** forged, replayed, or misdirected approvals — a decision meant for call A executed against call B, or a client-supplied response substituting arguments.
- **Demonstrated by:** langfuse (decide mutation accepts only IDs + boolean; tool name/args resolved from the persisted interrupt, "never client input", `web/src/features/in-app-agent/server/backgroundRunService.ts:379-400`); agent-framework (responses rebound against immutable recorded snapshots; unbound responses dropped with warning, `python/packages/core/agent_framework/_tools.py:2182-2213,2237-2239`); openai-agents-sdk (call-ID reuse for a different invocation raises `ModelBehaviorError`, `src/agents/run_context.py:344-352`; restored-but-unbound decisions force reapproval, `run_context.py:1308-1316`).
- **Why it works:** the decision cannot carry more authority than the recorded request; forgery tests prove dropped responses (`test_harness_tool_approval.py:708-742`) and replay suites prove cross-function/argument/label/session reuse fails (`test_security.py:814-1163`).
- **Copy when:** always, wherever a decision crosses a trust boundary (UI→server, resume→executor, history→loop).
- **Risky when:** skipped "temporarily" — openhands' bare `accept: boolean` against a loosely inferred pending event shows the degraded form (`conversation-confirmation-buttons.tsx:30-36`).

### P3. Narrow-by-default grants with opt-in stickiness

- **Problem:** broad grants ("always allow this tool") accumulate silently into global authority.
- **Demonstrated by:** openai-agents-sdk (default grant is one exact call ID; the broadest option, `always_approve`, still confines to one fingerprinted tool identity within one run; namespaced tools cannot be unlocked under their bare names, `tests/test_run_context_approvals.py:531-556`); agent-framework (one-shot occurrence default; standing rules narrowable three ways — per tool, per exact-argument-set where empty dict matches only zero-arg calls, per hosted `server_label`, `python/packages/core/agent_framework/_harness/_tool_approval.py:50-58,301-321,315`); langfuse (decision defaults to `"once"`; conversation grants confined to one tool in one conversation, `backgroundRunService.ts:396-400`).
- **Why it works:** the safe behavior is the zero-configuration behavior; widening requires an affirmative, auditable act.
- **Copy when:** designing any grant vocabulary. Start at occurrence granularity; make breadth a named parameter.
- **Cost:** operator friction — openai-agents-sdk notes long-lived automations must build their own cross-run grant store because sticky grants deliberately die with the run.

### P4. Compiler/test-enforced exhaustive policy classification

- **Problem:** a new sensitive tool added without a policy decision silently inherits whatever the default is.
- **Demonstrated by:** langfuse (every MCP tool must appear in the `"auto"`/`"approval"` map; exhaustiveness enforced by types plus runtime assertions against the registry, new tools fail compilation/tests until classified, `mcpPolicy.ts:22-26,27-360`); temporal (every API tagged in one hand-maintained map, and *unclassified* APIs fail closed in the default authorizer, `default_authorizer.go:55-57`); agent-framework (spec scenario-to-test matrix mapping ~30 approval invariants to named regressions, `docs/specs/004-python-function-calling-loop.md:445-474`).
- **Why it works:** turns policy coverage into a build-time invariant instead of a review-time hope.
- **Copy when:** your tool/API surface grows faster than policy review.
- **Limit:** temporal's `Access` tags are explicitly advisory — plugins may ignore them (`metadata.go:10-13`) — so exhaustiveness guarantees classification, not consumption.

### P5. Fail-closed decision semantics with loud errors

- **Problem:** ambiguous or corrupt gate inputs resolving to "allow".
- **Demonstrated by:** agent-framework (only strict `value is True` grants; non-bool deserializes as rejection; enforcement failures abort via re-raised `MiddlewareFailure` rather than laundering into tool-error text, `python/packages/core/agent_framework/_tools.py:1975-1977,1635-1641`); openai-agents-sdk (unparseable arguments skip the policy callable and force manual approval; crashing checkers degrade to requires-approval; invalid config raises `UserError`, `src/agents/util/_approvals.py:46-50`; `tool_planning.py:806-812`); opa (undefined decision → HTTP 500, not allow, `authorizer.go:134-138`); langfuse (malformed override header falls back to read-only, `web/src/pages/api/public/mcp/index.ts:203-216`); temporal (nil claims deny; unknown APIs deny, `default_authorizer.go:41-43,55-57`).
- **Why it works:** corruption and misconfiguration produce visible breakage instead of silent privilege escalation; opa pairs this with diagnostics like the token-auth-without-authz warning (`v1/runtime/runtime.go:680-682`).
- **Copy when:** universally — this is the cheapest pattern in the study.
- **Caveat:** fail-closed can masquerade as an outage; opa's report suggests a dedicated error code for the undefined case to speed triage (`authorizer.go:136`).

### P6. Revalidation-on-use kills stale grants

- **Problem:** standing grants outlive the conditions under which they were granted (role change, membership loss).
- **Demonstrated by:** langfuse — the worker rebuilds the tool policy from the *current* user role on every run, so a demoted user loses auto-approval even though the grant row persists, regression-tested (`worker/src/features/in-app-agent/executeInAppAgentRun.ts:335-344`; `tools.test.ts:6-24`); the ephemeral per-run API key adds a second lifetime bound by deleting itself at terminal state (`executeInAppAgentRun.ts:382-399`).
- **Contrast:** agent-framework core standing rules persist until session state is cleared, with no removal API found (grep for `revoke|expir` returned nothing, per its report); openai-agents-sdk mitigates structurally — resumed per-call decisions without a ledger binding are invalidated (`run_context.py:1308-1316`) — but sticky grants inside a long-paused run stay valid indefinitely.
- **Why it works:** decouples grant storage (durable, simple) from grant validity (checked fresh at use time).
- **Copy when:** grants outlive a single request and your platform already owns an identity/role source. Without a role source (pure libraries), structural binding (P2) is the fallback.

### P7. Independent layered gates across process boundaries

- **Problem:** any single enforcement point is a single point of compromise.
- **Demonstrated by:** langfuse — web validates ownership, the worker independently revalidates membership and conversation ownership at claim time ("nothing from enqueue time is trusted"), and the MCP endpoint separately enforces key-level allowlists (`executeInAppAgentRun.ts:196-257`; `registry.ts:170-189`); agent-framework stacks loop-level `ApprovalMode`, middleware, FIDES policy, and transport-edge lifecycle; temporal wraps authn→authz→router with code-level guards that override even an allowful authz policy (bundle-root write locks, `server.go:2521-2558`); opa strips unsafe builtins like `http.send` from server-side eval regardless of authz outcome (`server.go:996`).
- **Why it works:** exploitation requires simultaneous failure of independent layers written in different contexts.
- **Caution:** layers must share one vocabulary — langfuse keeps browser/web/worker on one shared parser module (`interrupts.ts:15-16`); divergence otherwise breeds drift like openhands' mock schema offering analyzer choices the adapter half doesn't recognize (`src/mocks/settings-handlers.ts:433-436` vs `agent-server-adapter.ts:611-614`).

### P8. Identity-fingerprinted scopes to defeat name collisions

- **Problem:** tools with equal names on different servers/instances would otherwise share grants.
- **Demonstrated by:** openai-agents-sdk (sticky decisions bind to a SHA-256 fingerprint over invocation type + canonical lookup key, or `server_label` + tool name for hosted MCP; cross-server collisions tested to not authorize, `src/agents/_tool_invocation.py:236-289`; `tests/test_run_context_approvals.py:207-243`); agent-framework (standing rules require `server_label` equality, regression-tested, `test_harness_tool_approval.py:1224`).
- **Why it works:** scope becomes a function of stable identity rather than spellable strings.
- **Gap:** agent-framework's *auto-approval rules* still match by bare name and its own documentation flags the collision class (`_file_access.py:1375-1382`) — evidence that retrofitting fingerprints onto a name-based scheme leaves seams.

## Key Differences

**Who approves — four distinct answers.** In the SDK sources the "who" is deliberately unmodeled: openai-agents-sdk delegates entirely to application code with no principal argument on approve/reject (explicitly searched, per its report), and agent-framework's only attribution is an optional free-text `reason` (`python/packages/core/agent_framework/_harness/_tool_approval.py:239-245`). Products own identity: langfuse records `decidedByUserId` on the decision event (`approvalEvents.ts:13`) and restricts v1 approval to the conversation owner (`backgroundRunService.ts:366-371`); openhands authenticates whoever holds the session key. Infrastructure replaces the human: in opa and temporal the *policy* approves, evaluated against JWT/mTLS-derived identity (`v1/server/identifier/tls.go:23-32`; `default_jwt_claim_mapper.go:76-110`). This is a library-vs-application constraint, not a quality difference — SDKs cannot impose an identity model they don't possess.

**Where decision state lives.** Transcript contents (agent-framework), versioned run-state JSON (openai-agents-sdk), a Postgres row plus a conversation-scoped grant column (langfuse, `schema.prisma:248-249`), nothing at all (temporal recomputes per request; opa caches evaluations, `server_test.go:5191-5330`), and a wire contract only (openhands). The durability requirement drives the choice: workflow-grade HITL demands persistence; high-throughput infrastructure demands statelessness.

**Default posture splits the group.** agent-framework makes its own dangerous surfaces require approval out of the box and guards the escape hatch (`acknowledge_unsafe=True` or raise, `shell/_tool.py:150-172`). The others gate conservatively only after configuration: temporal's unconfigured fallback grants admin to everyone (`noop_authorizer.go:12-14`), opa's authorization defaults off (`server.go:63-80`), openai-agents-sdk defaults `needs_approval=False` (`tool.py:486-492`), openhands defaults `confirmation_mode=false` (`settings.ts:13`). Langfuse is the hybrid: RBAC is unconditional, approval classes are curated constants. Whether this reflects maturity or product shape is debatable — libraries avoid imposing friction on hosts who may not want any gate; infrastructure optimizes adoption and relies on operator discipline plus warnings.

**Granularity ceiling differs by an order of magnitude.** openai-agents-sdk and agent-framework scope down to a single call occurrence and even exact argument sets; langfuse scopes to conversation × tool name × RBAC scope; temporal stops at API × namespace × role, documenting narrower resources (workflow types, task queues, Nexus endpoints) as future extension space with the hook fields plumbed but unused (`authorizer.go:22-23,30-33`); opa's ceiling is whatever the policy expresses — unlimited in principle, unevidenced in practice since granularity is invisible to the system itself; openhands offers exactly two knobs and one axis of narrowing (risk threshold).

**Revocation models.** Temporal and opa delegate revocation to credential/policy rotation — instant at next request once tokens expire or bundles swap, but with no kill switch for a specific issued authority (temporal's report: "no server-side session kill list"). Langfuse revokes implicitly via per-run RBAC revalidation. openai-agents-sdk revokes structurally via ledger binding. agent-framework offers batch replacement (a newly surfaced batch supersedes abandoned authority, `_tools.py:2141-2179`) but no explicit rule removal. Openhands has none.

**Enforcement visibility.** Five sources contain their own enforcement; openhands does not — its report repeatedly marks veto semantics as unverifiable-from-source, which directly caps its rating. This is worth stating as a study finding: a permission design whose enforcement you cannot inspect cannot be rated above "present."

## Tradeoffs

- **Durable approvals vs. attack surface and complexity.** Serializing pending authority (P1) buys resumable HITL but creates the replay/forgery problem that dominates the top sources' test suites — agent-framework's spec warns edits can duplicate side effects or replay stale authority (`docs/specs/004-python-function-calling-loop.md:31-33`), and openai-agents-sdk's candidate-key walk for legacy compatibility (`src/agents/run_context.py:1108-1235`) is called out by its own report as genuinely hard to audit. Source A uses transcript-content persistence because approvals must survive provider round-trips and history providers; Source B (openhands) uses an in-memory binary flag because a desktop-class frontend accepts losing idempotency on reload. The middle position — langfuse's DB row with CAS — costs transactional discipline but gets exactly-once semantics with far less parsing complexity than content-in-transcript.
- **Expressive policy engines vs. auditable catalogs.** opa gains tenant-definable logic over full request documents but loses a machine-readable permission inventory and ships no record of its own authorization decisions (`authorizer.go:116-132` uses a bare eval with no logger hook). Temporal and langfuse choose static, code-defined matrices (`common/api/metadata.go:70-208`; `mcpPolicy.ts:27-360`) — diffable, type-checkable, test-enforced — at the cost of requiring deploys for policy change and foreclosing tenant-defined policies. Neither choice is strictly superior; they answer different questions (who writes policy: operators vs. developers).
- **Safe defaults vs. adoption friction.** Deny-by-everywhere defaults maximize safety but push ergonomic burden onto integrators — agent-framework hosts must consciously configure MCP sampling callbacks and auto-rules to make agents usable (`_mcp.py:1494-1496`), while temporal/OPA/openhands let users start instantly and warn afterward. The study's cautionary extreme is temporal's noop claim mapper granting admin-for-all *silently as a zero value* (`claim_mapper.go:52-54`): a fail-open default dressed as "unset."
- **Name-based matching vs. identity-based matching.** Names keep rules portable across instances and readable in config (agent-framework auto-rules); fingerprints prevent collisions but leak abstraction into every serialized artifact (openai-agents-sdk's alias mirroring exists precisely to keep renamed tools working, `src/agents/run_context.py:888-996`). Agent-framework's documented collision warning versus openai-agents-sdk's collision tests is the cleanest A/B in the study.
- **Human-in-the-loop liveness vs. autonomy.** Any gated call freezes the whole run in openai-agents-sdk's uniform pause semantic (partial resolution supported, `docs/human_in_the_loop.md:57`), trading throughput for simplicity; agent-framework softens this with mixed-batch sibling pre-approval so ungated calls aren't blocked behind a visible request (`_tools.py:1796-1831`); langfuse parks only the affected run and lets the user send a new message, at the cost of superseding the parked approval (`runLifecycle.ts:162-174`). Infrastructure sources sidestep entirely by having no human step.

## Decision Guide

**Choose by product shape first:**

1. **Building an agent SDK/library** → copy the openai-agents-sdk / agent-framework core model: interruption items surfaced on results, decisions centralized in a serializable ledger bound to canonical invocation identity (P1+P2+P3+P5), fail-closed parsing everywhere. Leave "who approves" and TTL to hosts via hooks, but reserve metadata fields for them now (both 9s lack approver identity and TTL — learn from their gap list, not just their code).
2. **Building a SaaS product whose agent acts on users' behalf** → copy langfuse: RBAC filters availability before the model sees tools; approval never widens permissions; decisions are exactly-once CAS transactions; standing grants revalidate against current role every run; credentials are ephemeral per-run artifacts (P4+P6+P7). Add what langfuse lacks: a revoke/list UI for grants and a TTL column.
3. **Building infrastructure services (APIs, servers)** → copy temporal's seam with OPA's hardening: pluggable `Authorizer` over a static per-method metadata map, stateless per-request evaluation, cross-intent re-authorization for requests carrying embedded sub-actions (P4+P5+P7). Do not copy temporal's fail-open unset default; do copy OPA's startup type-checking of the security input contract and its misconfiguration warnings.
4. **Building a thin frontend over someone else's enforcement** → openhands' compilation layer is fine UX architecture, but require from your backend: a decision payload carrying the pending event's identity (not "the last event"), server-side idempotency for repeat submissions, and an audit record. If you cannot verify enforcement, treat the gate as decorative.

**Decision rules within a design:**

- Need human approval? Only if the operation is side-effecting and irreversible; otherwise prefer static policy (reads were auto-classified even in approval-heavy langfuse).
- Grant lifetime: occurrence < session/conversation < forever. Ship the narrowest as default and require an explicit parameter for each widening step (all three agent sources converge here).
- Sticky grants need either a TTL, a revocation API, or revalidation-on-use — pick at least one. Every source missing all three listed it as a residual risk.
- If your gate input comes from a client/UI, redesign so it carries only identifiers + decision (langfuse's zero-trust payloads); resolve everything else from persisted server state.

## Practical Tips

- **Make the approval decision parse strictly.** Accept only an explicit true/false; coerce nothing. Non-boolean `approved` values deserialize as rejection in agent-framework (`_types.py:1459-1461`) — a one-line policy that eliminates a whole ambiguity class.
- **Bind decisions to a recorded snapshot, not to a conversation turn.** Rebuild or verify the response against the immutable recorded request before executing (`_tools.py:2182-2213`); drop mismatches loudly.
- **Fingerprint tool identity for sticky scopes**: invocation kind + canonical lookup key (+ server label for remote surfaces). Test the collision cases explicitly (cross-server, namespaced-vs-bare-name — see openai-agents-sdk's suite, `tests/test_run_context_approvals.py:30,207-243,531-556`).
- **Classify every tool at rest.** Use a const map with type-enforced exhaustiveness and a runtime assertion against the registry, so adding a tool breaks the build until a human chooses `"auto"` or `"approval"` (langfuse, `mcpPolicy.ts:22-26`).
- **Write the adversarial tests first**: forged responses, replayed call IDs, changed arguments, escalated context labels, cross-session reuse. Both 9-score sources' ratings rest substantially on named tests for exactly these scenarios (`test_harness_tool_approval.py:708-742`; `test_security.py:814-1163`; `test_tool_approval_call_id_reuse.py`).
- **Surface denials observably**: WARNING logs on every denial (agent-framework, `_mcp.py:1442-1461`), denial counters with bounded cardinality (temporal, `interceptor.go:309-345`), and custom deny reasons returned to callers (opa, `authorizer.go:146-158`). Silent denials generate support tickets; logged denials generate fixes.
- **Warn on ineffective configuration**: authentication-without-authorization (OPA, `runtime.go:680-682`), confirmation-off-but-analyzer-set style contradictions, and root/over-privileged runtime checks encode security review as startup diagnostics.
- **Keep a disclosure-before-decision invariant**: detect the complete set of policy violations up front so one approval discloses the full risk set, and force fresh approval if the set changes (agent-framework FIDES layer, `security.py:1994-2060`).
- **Treat LLM-assessed risk as advisory metadata, never as the gate.** openhands' `security_risk` is the *model's own claim* about its action (`action-event.ts:43-49`); the gate must be the configured threshold evaluated server-side, and whether that happens must be verifiable in code you control.

## Anti-Patterns / Caution Signs

- **Fail-open zero values.** Temporal's unset claim mapper = admin-for-all (`claim_mapper.go:52-54`); OPA authorization defaults off; openhands' OSS permission hook is a hard-coded `return true` (`use-has-permission.ts:4-7`). Caution sign: the safe posture requires *opt-in* configuration that looks optional in docs.
- **Name-keyed auto-approval.** Any rule matching tools by string name carries the documented silent-collision hazard (`_file_access.py:1375-1382`). Caution sign: rules configurable by users that reference tools they didn't register.
- **Grants with neither TTL nor revocation nor revalidation.** Found in weakened form in agent-framework core (standing rules), openai-agents-sdk (long-paused runs), langfuse (grant rows), opa (tokens, and signatures ignoring `exp`/`nbf`, `verify.go:118-208`), temporal (no kill list). One instance is a backlog item; a portfolio-wide pattern is the dimension's biggest unsolved problem.
- **Binary decisions against loosely identified pending actions.** `accept: boolean` plus "last agent event" inference (openhands) means a status/event-stream disagreement attaches approval to the wrong action; memory-only duplicate suppression evaporates on reload (`event-message-store.ts:6-20`).
- **Advisory security metadata that enforcement may ignore.** Temporal's `Access` tags are documented non-binding (`metadata.go:10-13`); openhands' UNKNOWN-risk handling differs per analyzer path such that the safety envelope depends entirely on which analyzer string is configured (`agent-server-adapter.ts:600-604`). Caution sign: two code paths computing different safety outcomes from equivalent configurations.
- **Self-modifiable policy without provenance controls.** OPA's authz policy lives in the store it protects; whoever can write policies can rewrite their own permissions — demonstrated intentionally in its own tests (`server_test.go:5137-5162`); signed bundles are the mitigation but are opt-in (`plugins/bundle/config.go:175-182`).
- **Mock/schema drift hiding supported options.** openhands' mocked E2E schema cannot exercise two of four supported analyzers (`mocks/settings-handlers.ts:433-436` vs `agent-server-adapter.ts:611-614`) — tests pass while real capability is untested.
- **Auto-approved escape hatches.** langfuse's sandbox `read/write/edit/bash` skip the human-approval tier entirely (`mcpPolicy.ts:410-421`), concentrating all risk in sandbox isolation — acceptable only when the isolation boundary is independently engineered and named (its dev provider is literally called `dangerous-docker`).
- **Frontend-compiled safety policy without integrity checks.** A stale openhands build silently ships `NeverConfirm` while the user believes confirmation is on; no tie-back exists between sent policy and displayed settings.

## Notable Absences

- **Approver identity/RBAC is near-universally missing.** Only langfuse records who decided (`decidedByUserId`); temporal returns a principal in Allow results and embeds `auth_subject` in lifecycle events (`remote_cluster_lifecycle_events.go:177-190`) but has no human approvers. Both 9-score SDKs explicitly searched for approver/attribution concepts and found none — the clearest shared gap versus enterprise delegation models.
- **No maker/checker or delegated approval anywhere.** langfuse explicitly reports no second-person approval flows; no other source even has the vocabulary.
- **No TTL/expiry on standing grants in any agent source.** Pending *requests* expire (langfuse 24h, `tunables.ts:16-17`; AG-UI configurable windows, `_approval_lifecycle.py:242-260`), but granted authority does not time out in agent-framework core, openai-agents-sdk, langfuse grants, or openhands (which has no grant concept at all).
- **OPA never logs its own authorization decisions** — the logs-plugin pipeline masks/drops data events but the authorizer performs a bare eval with no logger hook (`authorizer.go:116-132`); operators have no built-in allow/deny history.
- **Openhands has no approval audit trail** and no in-repo test exercising the actual confirm/reject mutation round-trip; enforcement is wholly external to the studied tree.
- **Temporal has no persisted approval/grant artifact at all** — the word "approval" has zero hits in its tree — so dimension steps 3–4 (persistence, revocation mechanisms) evaluate to "delegated to credential lifetime" by design.
- **agent-framework's Go implementation has no approval layer** (searches returned no files), a parity gap inside a polyglot monorepo that mirrors Python/.NET models elsewhere.
- **Handoff/delegation is ungated in openai-agents-sdk** — no `needs_approval` exists on `Handoff`; delegation is gated only via `Agent.as_tool` (`handoffs/__init__.py:126-371`).

## Per-Source Notes

- **agent-framework (9)** — Most complete approval architecture in the study: content-type approvals, per-tool modes, opt-in middleware with scoped standing rules, FIDES information-flow policy, MCP sampling gate, and a server-owned AG-UI lifecycle, unified by a spec with a scenario-to-test matrix. Weaknesses: no TTL/revocation on core standing rules, no approver identity, name-based auto-rule collisions, Go stack absent.
- **langfuse (8)** — Best productized governance: RBAC-first availability, exhaustive classification registry, exactly-once CAS decisions, per-run revalidation killing stale grants, ephemeral per-run credentials, oversight webhooks on admin bypass. Weaknesses: no grant TTL/revoke API, sandbox bash auto-approved, approval history stranded in an event stream separate from the structured audit log, owner-only approvals block delegation.
- **opa (8)** — Strongest meta-hardening: schema type-checked authz input contract at startup and activation, fail-closed undefined handling, signed-bundle supply chain, live-reconfigurable decision refs. Weaknesses: unaudited authz decisions, no identity/token expiry, signatures ignoring temporal claims, off-by-default gating, self-rewritable policy absent deliberate signing adoption.
- **openai-agents-sdk (9)** — Deepest correctness engineering: canonical invocation ledger, fingerprinted sticky scopes, deterministic conflict rules, versioned persistence with compatibility corpus, fail-closed bias across five error classes, extensive dedicated suites. Weaknesses: legacy key-resolution branching hard to audit, no TTL, no approver identity, hosted-MCP server-side enforcement unverifiable from source.
- **openhands (6)** — Clean, typed, well-tested *configuration and UX* pipeline for a gate whose enforcement lives in another repo: policy derivation, layered risk surfacing, keyboard-first approval, duplicate-submission guard. Weaknesses: binary unscoped decisions, loose pending-action inference, memory-only idempotency, fail-open OSS stub, mock drift, no audit trail — most capped by unverifiable enforcement.
- **temporal (7)** — Exemplary stateless authorization seam: per-API metadata map, composable namespace bitmasks, cross-namespace command re-authorization, spoof-proof principal hygiene, table-driven decision-matrix tests, observable masked denials. Weaknesses: fail-open noop default, advisory access tags, network-trust internals with zero internal authz, stream blind spot, no grant artifact to revoke or expire.

## Open Questions

- Does provider-side enforcement back hosted-MCP approvals in openai-agents-sdk after `mcp_approval_response` is sent (`run_internal/tool_execution.py:1496-1505`)? Unverifiable from SDK source alone.
- Are openhands' repeat confirmation submissions idempotent server-side? The client guard is memory-only and the enforcement repo was out of boundary.
- Is there any intended revocation API for agent-framework core standing rules, or is clearing session state the only mechanism? Grep evidence says the latter; intent undocumented.
- Would Temporal Cloud add approval workflows over the OSS seam? Not assessable from the OSS tree.
- Do langfuse plan tiers alter approval classification (vs. merely gating agent availability)? Evidence shows entitlements affect availability only.
- Should the study treat "policy approves" (OPA/temporal) and "human approves" (the other four) as one dimension at all, or split 08.02 into machine-authorization vs. human-approval sub-dimensions? The evidence here suggests the convergence is structural (fail-closed, binding, scoping) while the actors differ fundamentally.
